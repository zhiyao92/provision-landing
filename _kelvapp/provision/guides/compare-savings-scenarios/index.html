How to compare savings scenarios without fooling yourself

Compare hypothetical savings plans by changing one assumption at a time, recording inputs and keeping calculator estimates in perspective.

By Kelvapp · 7 November 2026 · 3 min read

A savings calculator can produce a beautiful number from some very optimistic assumptions. The number is not necessarily wrong. It may just belong to a version of your life where every month goes perfectly and nothing expensive ever breaks.

A more useful approach is to compare a few clearly labelled scenarios. Provision includes savings and household-finance calculators with live results, calculation history and tools for keeping selected results. Here is a practical way to use those features for a hypothetical planning exercise, without treating an estimate as a promise.

Write the question before entering numbers

Make the question specific enough that a result can answer it. “How much might I set aside over twelve months?” is easier to explore than “Am I doing well with money?” The second question involves far more than a calculator can know.

For a simple example, imagine a fictional target of 2,400 currency units. With no starting balance, no interest and twelve equal contributions, the arithmetic is 200 units per month. That is an illustration, not a suggested contribution or a judgment about what anyone can afford.

Create a baseline you can explain

Choose the relevant calculator and check the units before typing. Is the period in months or years? Is the contribution monthly or annual? If the tool includes a rate, what period does that rate describe? Small unit mistakes can produce impressively unhelpful results.

Write down the starting amount, contribution, duration and any rate assumption. Use values you intend to test, not values selected to make the answer look comforting. Select the appropriate region where relevant, and check the dates and linked authorities for any regional rules you use.

Change one assumption at a time

Keep your baseline, then make a second scenario with only one difference. You might change the hypothetical monthly contribution while leaving the duration unchanged. In a third scenario, restore the original contribution and change the duration instead.

This makes the comparison readable. When every input changes at once, you cannot easily tell which assumption moved the result. Provision updates calculations as inputs change, so pause long enough to notice which value you are testing before moving on to the next.

Include an inconvenient version

Consider an alternative that represents less predictable circumstances, such as a smaller contribution or a later start. You are exploring how dependent the result is on your assumptions, not forecasting what will happen.

Keep the model’s limits visible. A particular calculator may not represent changing contributions, fees, inflation, tax treatment or variable returns in the way your real situation requires. Check what the selected tool actually includes. If an important factor is absent, record that omission instead of quietly assuming it has been handled.

Save the assumptions alongside the answer

Provision’s automatic history helps you revisit previous calculations, while My Files lets you keep selected results deliberately. A result card or PDF can give you a record to review later. Before sharing one, inspect it for personal financial details and make sure the recipient actually needs them.

Alongside each saved version, keep your own short explanation: what question you asked, which input changed, when you ran it and what the estimate leaves out. A bare total is much less useful a month later than a result with understandable assumptions.

Know when the calculator has finished its job

The output can help you frame questions. It cannot decide suitability, establish affordability or replace current information from a provider, authority or qualified adviser. Verify consequential decisions with the appropriate source.
